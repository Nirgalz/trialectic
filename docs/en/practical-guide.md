# Practical Guide to the Trialectic Method
## Analyze a situation, understand its mechanisms, and explore solutions

### Who is this guide for?

This guide is intended for anyone who wants to understand a complex situation better, address a problem, examine different possibilities, or deepen their reasoning.

No specialist knowledge is required.

You can use it on your own, with other people, or with artificial intelligence.

It offers a gradual workflow that should be adapted to the situation's complexity. It is not a scientifically validated protocol.

## Before starting: choose a question

Begin by stating what you are trying to understand.

For example:

- Why is an association struggling to maintain its activities?
- How could someone gain access to a new professional activity?
- What changes might improve a public service?
- Why are two groups unable to reach an agreement?

A useful question is specific enough to guide the analysis without presupposing the problem's causes or its solution.

Instead of asking only “How can we obtain more funding?”, you might ask “What is preventing our association from maintaining its activities, and what possibilities might help overcome these difficulties?”

The second formulation allows you to examine other causes and solutions besides funding.

Keep your original question: you can reformulate it if the analysis reveals that it is incomplete or poorly framed.

# Stage One — Understand the Subject

## 1. Identify what you are analyzing

The first stage is to choose the **Main Subject**.

It may be an individual, a group, a technical object, a system, or a semantic object.

Then specify what you want to understand about it.

**Example**

*Question: How could a library maintain a service that helps people complete administrative procedures online?*

The Main Subject is the library. The state being examined is the continued provision of the service.

The question does not necessarily concern every activity of the library. The analysis may focus on properties and relationships relevant to this service.

## 2. Delimit its Environment

Identify elements that may influence the situation.

These may include other people, organizations, material resources, rules, technical systems, or relationships between them.

In our example, several Objects may be relevant:

- Library staff.
- Residents who use the service.
- The municipality.
- Local associations.
- Computer equipment and networks.
- Rules applicable to administrative procedures and assistance.

Not all of these elements necessarily have the same importance.

Start with those that could change the situation being examined.

**Check:** Are some elements constituents of the Subject or part of its functioning? Do others intervene only through its Environment? Are there indirect influences?

You can broaden the analysis if new dependencies emerge.

## 3. Specify the time context

An analysis should state when it applies.

In particular, distinguish:

- The current situation.
- Changes that have already occurred.
- Changes being considered.
- The time horizon over which their consequences will be examined.

A solution may be feasible today but no longer feasible in six months.

Conversely, a Potentiality that is not presently accessible may become accessible after training, investment, or a change in the Environment.

**Expected result of Stage One:** a sufficiently precise description of the Subject, the question, the context, and the principal Objects concerned.

# Stage Two — Examine necessities and potentialities

You must now choose what to focus on: a **NEED** or a **CAN**.

This choice establishes the analysis's *Focus*.

A problem can be approached through an unsatisfied necessity. Another analysis may begin with a Potentiality that you wish to understand or develop.

## 4. Identify NEEDs

A NEED is a necessity attributable to an Object, required for the existence, maintenance, or realization of a specified state of that Object.

To identify relevant NEEDs, examine the following questions.

**What state are we trying to maintain or achieve?**

Describe that state precisely enough.

**What is actually necessary for that state?**

Distinguish indispensable elements from those that are merely desirable or favorable.

**Are these necessities satisfied?**

For each NEED, indicate whether satisfaction is established, lack of satisfaction is established, or information is missing.

**For how long must they be satisfied?**

Some necessities must be satisfied at a particular moment. Others require continuous satisfaction or satisfaction at specified times.

### Example

To provide a digital assistance service every Wednesday for three months, the library might need:

| Candidate NEED | Status to check |
|---|---|
| Have an appropriate space | Satisfied or not? |
| Have functional equipment and a connection | Satisfied during every session? |
| Ensure that a qualified person is present | Satisfied for all time slots? |
| Comply with applicable confidentiality requirements | Conditions identified and satisfied? |

These are candidates for examination. Whether each is necessary and whether it is satisfied must be confirmed in the context being studied.

A NEED can be satisfied without any new intervention. If the relevant necessities are already met, the analysis may examine how to maintain them, how they could change, or conclude that no new action is currently required.

## 5. Identify CANs

A CAN is a Potentiality attributable to an Object.

Begin by listing the Subject's Potentialities that are relevant to the analysis.

Then examine those of other Objects in the Environment that could contribute to the situation.

**Useful questions:**

- What could the Subject potentially accomplish?
- What resources, knowledge, or properties make this possible?
- Which Potentialities of other Objects could contribute?
- Do some Potentialities exist without being currently accessible?
- Could new Potentialities be discovered or developed?

### Example

For the digital assistance service:

| Object | Candidate CAN |
|---|---|
| Library | Organize assistance sessions |
| Staff | Assist users with administrative procedures |
| Partner association | Provide specialist assistants |
| Municipality | Make certain resources available |
| Library and partners | Organize volunteer training |

Be careful: a Potentiality attributed to a group should not automatically be attributed to every member, or vice versa.

Similarly, a Potentiality does not necessarily involve autonomous action by the Object to which it is attributed.

## 6. Examine relationships between NEEDs and CANs

Look for possible relationships between the identified necessities and Potentialities that are available or could become available.

The same necessity may be satisfied through several pathways.

For example, a competent assistant could be provided by library staff, trained volunteers, or a specialist partner.

However, these proposed relationships must be checked.

Distinguish four questions:

1. **Correspondence:** Is there a relevant reason to think that this CAN could address the NEED?
2. **Contribution:** Could its Actualization actually favor the satisfaction of the NEED?
3. **Sufficiency:** Would this contribution, alone or combined with others, satisfy the NEED under the contemplated conditions?
4. **Scenario-relative necessity:** Is this contribution indispensable to the scenario being examined, even if other scenarios could do without it?

A plausible correspondence is not necessarily a demonstrated contribution.

A useful contribution is not necessarily sufficient.

And what is indispensable in one scenario may not be indispensable in another.

**Expected result of Stage Two:** an initial representation of necessities, Potentialities, and relationships that could explain the problem or open up new possibilities.

# Stage Three — Explore possible Actualizations

The third stage examines the conditions under which certain Potentialities can be implemented, then builds and compares several scenarios.

## 7. Identify action conditions

For each CAN being considered, look for the conditions that would allow it to contribute in the situation being examined.

These conditions may be internal to the Subject, depend on its Environment, or arise from relationships between several Objects.

Consider the CAN of organizing a session staffed by library employees.

Implementing it might require:

- Staff availability at the scheduled times.
- Skills needed to assist service users.
- A work organization compatible with other staff duties.
- Access to the required equipment.

A missing condition may lead you to examine another CAN that could help satisfy it.

For example, insufficient skills might suggest prior training.

But that training has its own action conditions: trainers, time, resources, and possibly permissions.

The analysis may thus reveal **dependencies between several Actualizations**.

### Assess Power

For a specific Actualization, Power is positive when at least one complete pathway of sufficient conditions is satisfied.

It is negative when those conditions are not met.

If the available information does not allow a conclusion, report an indeterminate assessment.

Do not confuse a CAN's ability to contribute to one particular action with the satisfaction of every condition required to undertake a broader Actualization.

Above all, **positive Power does not guarantee that the action undertaken will succeed**.

## 8. Build several Resolution Scenarios

Using the NEEDs, CANs, and conditions you have identified, develop several materially different scenarios.

A scenario may comprise several successive, simultaneous, or conditional Actualizations.

For the library, we can consider three possibilities.

### Scenario A — Reorganize in-house sessions

The library tries to maintain the service with its own staff.

It examines possible timetable changes, available skills, and effects on other activities.

### Scenario B — Cooperate with an association

An association could provide staff for some sessions.

This scenario depends on the association's Potentialities and NEEDs, as well as the conditions for cooperation between the organizations.

### Scenario C — Combine internal and external resources

The library retains some sessions and works with a partner for the others.

It could also develop volunteers' skills with a view to a subsequent evolution of the service.

This third possibility may involve dependencies and coordination difficulties that differ from the first two.

### Look for additional pathways

Do not limit your thinking to solutions that are immediately accessible.

When you identify an obstacle, ask:

*What change would make this possibility accessible?*

A CAN that is not currently actionable may remain a candidate if its missing conditions can be identified and potentially fulfilled.

If there is a circular dependency, examine whether another Potentiality could provide the conditions needed to initiate the process.

However, do not consider an obstacle resolved simply because a hypothetical solution has been imagined.

## 9. Compare scenarios

For each scenario, examine the following dimensions:

| Dimension | What to examine |
|---|---|
| Feasibility | Conditions met, missing, or uncertain |
| Complexity | Practical difficulties, coordination, and dependencies |
| Cost | Financial, human, and material resources |
| Temporality | Delays, duration of effects, continuity requirements |
| Expected effectiveness | Anticipated contribution to satisfying the NEED or achieving the objective |
| Consequences | Favorable and unfavorable effects on all relevant Objects |
| Uncertainty | Assumptions, available evidence, and missing information |

### Applying the comparison to our example

The three scenarios can be examined qualitatively:

| Scenario | Main conditions to verify | Points requiring attention |
|---|---|---|
| A. Internal reorganization | Availability, skills, work organization | Effects on staff members' other duties |
| B. Partnership | Partner availability, agreement, resources | External dependency and continuity of service |
| C. Combined arrangement | Coordination, allocation of responsibilities, resources | Management complexity and risks of discontinuity |

This comparison does not establish that any one scenario is better than the others.

It shows what information would be needed for a more complete evaluation.

One scenario may be less costly but harder to organize. Another may be quicker to implement while creating a lasting dependency.

A third may be more robust in the long term without meeting immediate constraints.

The consequences for staff, residents, partners, and the library's other activities must also be evaluated.

### Examine indirect effects

A change in work organization may affect staff availability for other services.

A partnership may open up new possibilities for cooperation.

Training volunteers may create new Potentialities but also require ongoing support.

These effects may in turn influence other elements of the system.

Always distinguish:

- Direct and indirect effects.
- Immediate and delayed effects.
- Favorable, unfavorable, or neutral effects according to the criteria used.
- Their magnitude, probability when it can be estimated, or degree of possibility in other cases.

Anticipated effects should remain hypotheses until they have been sufficiently verified.

## 10. Check the strength of the analysis

Before concluding, examine the claims on which your analysis rests.

For each important claim, ask:

**What do we know?** Which facts, documents, observations, or arguments justify this claim?

**What is the quality of that evidence?** Is it relevant to the exact question? How was it produced? Can it be checked? Do several sources rely on the same original information?

**What other explanations are possible?** Does the available evidence distinguish between competing hypotheses?

**What do we not know?** Which additional information could materially change the assessment?

A deduction that is coherent with the model's assumptions is not necessarily proof that those assumptions accurately describe reality.

Do not turn a qualitative assessment into a numerical probability without statistical justification.

When the stakes are high, verification requirements should be proportionate to the potential consequences.

### Must you select a scenario?

Not necessarily.

You may conclude that several scenarios deserve further investigation, that none is yet sufficiently documented, or that decisive information is missing.

If a decision must be made, state the criteria guiding the choice.

A recommendation may then be made conditionally, without claiming that it is universally the best solution.

# Return to the Subject — Reassess and continue

## 11. Examine changes

After an effective Actualization, return to the Main Subject.

What has changed?

Are some NEEDs now satisfied? Have new necessities emerged?

Have Potentialities been developed, modified, or lost?

Have the conditions for other Actualizations changed?

Has the transformation affected other Objects?

In our example, an implemented partnership could change how the library is organized, the partner's activities, and the assistance possibilities available to residents.

These transformations should be examined through their observed effects, distinguishing those that can actually be attributed to the intervention.

### Do not confuse simulation with reality

When you imagine several scenarios, you examine several hypothetical developments of the Subject and its Environment.

These developments must remain separate.

A consequence anticipated in Scenario A should not be treated as already achieved in Scenario B.

And no simulation should be confused with a transformation that has actually taken place.

The analysis may also change simply because you have obtained better information, even when the real situation has not changed.

## 12. Know when to investigate further or stop

A systemic analysis could theoretically continue indefinitely, because every Object has numerous relationships with other Objects.

The Trialectic Method therefore uses a principle of recursive relevance.

Continue exploring when additional effects or dependencies could materially change your understanding, your scenario evaluation, or the consequences for the Objects concerned.

You can limit exploration when its value becomes insufficient relative to the complexity and resources it requires.

Nevertheless, do not overlook unlikely but potentially major risks.

**Ending an analysis does not mean every possibility has been examined.** It means its scope and limits have been made explicit.

# Advice for using the Method

### Start with what is relevant

You do not need to describe every possible Object, NEED, and CAN.

Begin with the elements that matter to your question and enrich the analysis as needed.

### Be willing to reformulate the original question

If the analysis reveals a more fundamental difficulty, it may be appropriate to change the Focus or even choose another Subject.

This redirection is not a failure of the analysis: it can be one of its principal results.

### Do not confuse possibility with certainty

A Potentiality can be real without the conditions for its Actualization being satisfied.

A possible Actualization can fail.

And a plausible consequence is not a demonstrated consequence.

### Distinguish analysis from value judgments

Identifying what could happen is not enough to determine whether it is desirable.

When describing a consequence as favorable or unfavorable, specify for whom and according to which criteria.

### Use Semantics when it adds value

For some problems, clarifying a word, objective, belief, or representation can profoundly change the analysis.

In other cases, this perspective contributes little additional information.

Its use should remain guided by relevance.

### Keep uncertainties visible

Missing information is neither proof of impossibility nor a reason to invent an answer.

It is preferable to document what remains unknown and identify useful checks.

## Conclusion

The Trialectic Method proposes a process that involves:

1. Identifying and contextualizing a Subject.
2. Examining a NEED or CAN and its relationships with other Objects.
3. Studying the conditions for Actualization and several possible scenarios.
4. Comparing scenarios, their consequences, and their uncertainties.
5. Returning to the Subject to reassess the situation and continue the analysis when relevant.

The result is not necessarily a decision or a solution.

It may be a better understanding of the problem, the discovery of a Potentiality, the identification of a previously overlooked dependency, or the clarification of a disagreement.

**A successful analysis is not only one that leads to action: it can also be one that helps us better understand what is necessary, what is possible, and what different transformations might produce.**
